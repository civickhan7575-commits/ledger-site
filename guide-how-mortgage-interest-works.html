How mortgage interest actually works

If you've ever looked at your mortgage statement in year one and wondered why your balance barely moved, you're not imagining it. Almost every fixed-rate loan is front-loaded with interest — here's why.

The payment stays the same, the split doesn't

Your monthly payment is fixed for the life of the loan. But inside that fixed number, the split between interest and principal shifts every single month. Early on, your loan balance is at its highest, so the interest charged on it is also at its highest — meaning most of your payment covers interest, and only a small slice chips away at what you actually owe.

Why it flips over time

As your balance slowly drops, the interest portion shrinks too, since interest is calculated on whatever you still owe. That leaves more of your fixed payment free to go toward principal. On a typical 30-year loan, this crossover — where principal starts outpacing interest — usually happens around year 15 to 18, not year one.

Why this matters

Two practical takeaways: first, if you sell or refinance in the first several years, you'll have built up far less equity than the payment count alone suggests. Second, even small extra payments toward principal early in the loan have an outsized effect — because you're cutting the balance interest gets calculated on for years to come.
